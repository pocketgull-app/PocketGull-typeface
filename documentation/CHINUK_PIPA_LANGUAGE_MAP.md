## 1. Historical Provenance: From Mimeograph to Tribal Revitalization

### The Pacific Northwest Lingua Franca (Chinuk Wawa)
**Chinuk Wawa** (Chinook Jargon) emerged along the lower Columbia River basin, Willamette Valley, Puget Sound, and the Pacific Northwest coast. It served as a rich, expressive bridge language uniting diverse Indigenous nations—including Chinookan, Lower Chinook, Nuu-chah-nulth, Salishan, and Sahaptian peoples—alongside French Canadian *voyageurs*, Hawaiian *Kanakas*, and English and American traders.

### Father Jean-Marie Le Jeune & The 1891 Kamloops Phenomenon
In 1891, at the St. Louis Mission near Kamloops, British Columbia, Roman Catholic Oblate missionary Father Jean-Marie Le Jeune observed that the Secwepemc (Shuswap) and Nlaka'pamux (Thompson) people found Latin alphabet orthography frustrating and inconsistent. Different colonial traders spelled words arbitrarily, creating widespread reading barriers.

Le Jeune had learned the shorthand system created in 1860 by French priest Émile Duployé. Because Duployan shorthand was strictly **phonetic** and based on elementary geometric strokes (straight lines, arcs, and circles), Le Jeune adapted it to Chinuk Wawa. 

The results were astonishing: Indigenous elders and children learned to read and write fluent Chinuk Wawa within a matter of **days or weeks**. 

### The *Kamloops Wawa* Newspaper (1891–1923)
In May 1891, Le Jeune purchased an Edison mimeograph machine and began hand-writing and stencil-printing the monthly newspaper ***Kamloops Wawa*** ("The Kamloops Talk"). 

For over three decades:
- Thousands of copies circulated across British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and Alaska.
- Indigenous community members wrote letters to one another, recorded community councils, transcribed traditional oral histories, printed hymns, and exchanged medical advice.
- The writing system was affectionately called **Chinuk Pipa** (*Chinuk* = Chinook, *Pipa* = paper, writing, letter, book).

### Contemporary Stewardship: Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde
Today, Chinuk Wawa is a recognized living Indigenous language, safeguarded and actively revitalized by the **Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde** in western Oregon. Through immersion preschools, adult certification programs, and the landmark *Chinuk Wawa: kakwa nsayka ulman-tilixam laska munk-kemteks nsayka* dictionary (2012), Grand Ronde elders and scholars continue to teach the language of the ancestors.

### C. Chinuk Pipa Punctuation & Affixes

- **𛲟** (`U+1BC9F`) — **DUPLOYAN PUNCTUATION CHINOOK FULL STOP**: A distinctive cross or encircled saltire used systematically in *Kamloops Wawa* to mark the end of sentences and prayer clauses.
- **𛲞** (`U+1BC9E`) — **DUPLOYAN DOUBLE MARK**: Indicates syllable or word reduplication (essential for Chinuk Wawa reduplications: *heehee*, *tumtum*, *muckamuck*).
- **𛲜** (`U+1BC9C`) — **DUPLOYAN SIGN O WITH CROSS**: Used for sacred and ceremonial markers.
- **𛲀**–**𛲈** (`U+1BC80`–`U+1BC88`) — **Duployan High Affixes**: Diacritics indicating tone, elision, or grammatical particles.

## 4. Typographic Implementation in PocketGull

PocketGull synthesizes Duployan and Chinuk Pipa glyphs across all 4 superfamily fonts:

1. **`PocketGull-Fineliner`**: Light 400 weight capturing Father Le Jeune’s original steel-nib mimeograph stencils.
2. **`PocketGull-Bold`**: Robust 700 weight for clear roadside and clinical signage.
3. **`PocketGull-Chiseltip`**: Heavy 900 weight preserving the authentic angled felt marker geometry.
4. **`PocketGullMono-Regular`**: Centered on fixed 600 UPM advance for terminal displays, serial EHR monitors, and clinical telemetric HUDs.
